Water Sampling & Testing

A comprehensive guide to understanding water quality — from the science behind the parameters to the practical skills of sampling, testing, and interpreting results.

Why do we test water?

Water is essential for life, ecosystems, agriculture, and industry. Testing tells us about its health, safety, and suitability for different uses. It's the foundation of good water management.

Monitor change over time

Regular testing builds a baseline and tracks trends. This helps us see if a waterway is improving, declining, or stable — and whether management actions are working.

Example: Testing a river monthly for nutrients can reveal gradual increases from agricultural runoff, allowing early intervention.
Detect pollution & contamination

Testing can identify harmful substances — heavy metals, pesticides, excess nutrients, or pathogens. It's often the first step in investigating an environmental incident.

Example: After a fish kill, water samples help identify the cause: low dissolved oxygen, toxic algae, or chemical spill.
Compliance & regulations

Many industries and land managers must meet water quality standards set by law. Testing proves that water discharged from a site, or used for irrigation, meets legal requirements.

Protect human & ecosystem health

We test drinking water to ensure it's safe. We test natural waterways to ensure they support healthy fish, frogs, and plants. It's about protecting both people and the environment.

Water quality in the Australian context

Australia's unique challenges
  • Variable climate: Droughts and floods affect water quality dramatically.
  • Agriculture: Extensive farming can lead to nutrient runoff and salinity.
  • Urban development: Stormwater carries pollutants from roads and buildings.
  • Mining: Can introduce heavy metals and acid drainage.
  • Climate change: Warming waters, altered flow patterns, and increased bushfire runoff affect water quality.
National standards & guidelines

Australia has robust water quality guidelines to protect our waterways:

  • ANZECC 2000 — Australian and New Zealand Guidelines for Fresh and Marine Water Quality. Provides trigger values for ecosystem protection.
  • Australian Drinking Water Guidelines — health-based limits for drinking water.
  • State-based regulations — each state has specific water quality policies (e.g., Tasmanian Water Quality Guidelines).
Key takeaway: Water testing isn't just about collecting data — it's about understanding and protecting our water resources. Every test result tells a story about the health of that water body.

What we measure: key water quality parameters

These are the core parameters you'll test in the field. Each one tells us something different about the water's condition.

Temperature (°C)

What it is: A measure of how hot or cold the water is.

Why it matters: Temperature affects every other parameter. It influences oxygen levels, the toxicity of pollutants, and the survival of fish and invertebrates. Many species have a preferred temperature range.

How to measure: Using a thermometer or a multi-parameter probe. Submerge the sensor and wait for the reading to stabilise.

Expected ranges: Freshwater streams 5–25°C (seasonal), marine 12–24°C, lakes vary with depth.

pH (acidity/alkalinity)

What it is: The scale from 0 to 14 — 7 is neutral, below 7 is acidic, above 7 is alkaline.

Why it matters: pH influences chemical reactions in water. Many aquatic organisms are sensitive to pH changes. Extremes (below 5 or above 9) can be harmful. pH also affects the toxicity of metals and ammonia.

How to measure: Using a pH meter (electrode) or colour-change test strips. Calibrate the meter before use with buffer solutions.

Expected ranges: Freshwater 6.5–8.0, marine 7.5–8.5, estuaries variable.

Electrical Conductivity (EC) & Salinity

What it is: A measure of how well water conducts electricity, which relates to the amount of dissolved salts (ions). Often reported as salinity in parts per thousand (ppt).

Why it matters: EC tells us about the mineral content. High salinity can stress freshwater species; low salinity is important for estuaries. It helps identify saltwater intrusion or agricultural runoff.

How to measure: Using a conductivity sensor (often part of a multi-parameter sonde).

Expected ranges: Freshwater 0–500 µS/cm, brackish 500–5000, marine ~53,000 µS/cm (≈35 ppt).

Dissolved Oxygen (DO)

What it is: The amount of oxygen gas dissolved in the water, measured in mg/L or % saturation.

Why it matters: Fish and other aquatic life need oxygen to survive. Low DO can cause fish kills. DO levels fluctuate with temperature, plant activity, and pollution. Cold water holds more oxygen than warm water.

How to measure: Using a DO probe (optical or electrochemical). Ensure the probe is calibrated and free of bubbles.

Expected ranges: Healthy water 80–110% saturation (≈6–9 mg/L). Below 60% (≈4 mg/L) is stressful for many species.

Turbidity (clarity)

What it is: A measure of water clarity, affected by suspended particles like silt, clay, or algae. Reported in Nephelometric Turbidity Units (NTU).

Why it matters: High turbidity reduces light penetration, which can affect plant growth and fish feeding. It can also carry pollutants attached to sediment particles.

How to measure: Using a turbidity tube (visual), Secchi disk (in lakes), or a turbidity sensor. Always collect sample without disturbing sediment.

Expected ranges: Clear streams <5 NTU, turbid rivers 10–50 NTU, estuaries variable, lakes <10 NTU (clear) to >100 NTU (algal blooms).

Nutrients – Nitrate & Phosphate

What they are: Nitrate (NO₃⁻) and phosphate (PO₄³⁻) are essential plant nutrients, but in excess they cause problems.

Why they matter: High nutrient levels cause eutrophication — excessive algal growth (blooms) that can deplete oxygen and produce toxins. Measuring nutrients helps identify sources like fertiliser runoff, sewage, or animal waste.

How to measure: Using colourimetric test kits (drop tests or strips) or collecting samples for laboratory analysis.

Expected ranges: Natural waters: nitrate <1 mg/L, phosphate <0.05 mg/L. Elevated levels indicate pollution.

Additional parameters you may encounter

Total Dissolved Solids (TDS)

Similar to EC — a measure of all dissolved minerals. High TDS can affect taste and aquatic life.

Algae & cyanobacteria

Identified and counted under a microscope. Certain species produce toxins harmful to animals and humans.

Heavy metals

Trace metals like lead, mercury, arsenic, copper — toxic at low concentrations, often from mining or industrial sources.

Bacteriological indicators

E. coli and Enterococci indicate faecal contamination — important for drinking water and swimming safety.

Equipment, methods & best practice

Multi-parameter sonde

A single device with multiple sensors to measure temperature, pH, EC, DO, and turbidity simultaneously.

How to use: Calibrate before fieldwork (use standard solutions). Lower the probe into the water (avoid stirring sediment). Wait for readings to stabilise (usually 30–60 seconds). Record each value.

Care: Rinse with distilled water after use. Store sensors with moist pads. Replace caps and batteries as needed.

Tip: Always calibrate pH and DO meters on the day of sampling. Temperature and EC are more stable but should still be checked regularly.
Turbidity tube & Secchi disk

Turbidity tube: A clear tube with a black-and-white pattern on the base. Fill with sample water. Look down the tube and slowly add water until the pattern disappears — read the NTU scale at the water level.

Secchi disk: A black-and-white disk lowered into the water until it vanishes. Record the depth. Used in lakes and clear water.

Care: Clean and dry the tube after each use. Keep the disk clean and the rope tangle-free.

Refractometer (salinity)

A handheld optical instrument that measures salinity (ppt) or specific gravity.

How to use: Calibrate with distilled water (should read 0). Place 2–3 drops of sample on the prism window, close the cover, and look through the eyepiece toward a light source. Read where the boundary line crosses the scale.

Care: Rinse with clean fresh water after use, dry the prism, and store in a padded case.

Nutrient test kits (nitrate & phosphate)

Simple colour-change tests. Follow the kit instructions carefully — add the reagent, wait the specified time, and compare the colour to a chart.

Tips: Check expiry dates before use. Use clean, dry vials. Record the result immediately.

Pro tip: Always test a blank (distilled water) with your kit to ensure the reagents are working correctly.

General field sampling techniques

  • Label everything — write site, date, time, and test on each container before you collect the sample.
  • Rinse containers — rinse bottles 2–3 times with the site water before filling.
  • Avoid contamination — don't touch the inside of lids or the sample water with dirty hands.
  • Keep samples cool & dark — store in an insulated esky with ice packs to slow biological change.
  • Record observations — note weather, water colour, smell, and any visible algae or animals. This context is valuable.
  • Use appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE) — gloves, safety glasses, and appropriate footwear.

Planning a sampling program & staying safe

Choosing your sites

Your assessment requires samples from at least three different water bodies — for example, a freshwater river, a marine/estuarine site, and a farm dam or lake. This gives you a broad understanding of water quality variation.

Before you go, confirm site access, check tides (for marine sites), and think about safety.

Good practice: Choose sites that are representative of the water body — avoid areas near drains, stagnant pools, or under overhanging trees where debris accumulates.
Risk assessment essentials

Every field trip needs a risk assessment. Consider these common hazards:

  • Working near water — wear a life jacket, use a buddy system, avoid slippery banks.
  • Slips, trips & falls — wear sturdy footwear, keep work areas tidy.
  • Exposure to elements — sun protection (hat, sunscreen, long sleeves), hydration, and weather-appropriate clothing.
  • Bites & stings — be aware of snakes, spiders, and insects. Wear protective clothing and use repellent.
  • Chemical hazards — use test kits in a ventilated area, wear gloves and safety glasses, and follow SDS (Safety Data Sheets) for disposal.
  • Manual handling — carry equipment in packs, use proper lifting techniques for heavy coolers.
Weather and water conditions

Weather affects water quality. For example:

  • A hot, still day can lead to algal blooms and low oxygen.
  • A storm can increase turbidity and wash nutrients into waterways.
  • Rainfall after a dry spell can flush accumulated pollutants.

Always check the forecast before you go. High winds, heavy rain, or extreme heat may make fieldwork unsafe or invalidate results. Be prepared to reschedule.

Pro tip: Record weather conditions (temperature, cloud cover, wind, recent rainfall) on your data sheet — it helps explain unexpected results.

Collecting, storing & dispatching samples

Sample collection techniques

Surface samples: Use a clean container or a sampling pole. Submerge just below the surface, away from the bank.

Subsurface samples: Use a Niskin bottle or pump sampler. Record the depth.

Dissolved nutrients: Filter through a 0.45µm syringe filter immediately into a 50mL tube. This removes particles that can change nutrient levels during storage.

Algal samples: Fill a 500mL plastic jar to the shoulder. Add Lugols preservative (1% v/v — that's 5mL per 500mL) to preserve the sample for identification and counting.

Bacterial samples: Use sterile containers, collect aseptically, and keep cold. Deliver to lab within 24 hours.

Preservation & storage

Keep it cool: Place samples in an insulated esky with ice packs as soon as they're collected. This slows biological and chemical change.

Freeze for later: If you can't get samples to the lab on the same day, freeze them (for nutrients) within 24 hours. Don't overfill containers — allow room for expansion.

Holding times (Analytical Services Tasmania):

  • Total nutrients: 28 days (frozen)
  • Dissolved nutrients (with nitrite): 2 days (or freeze, 28 days)
  • Algal samples (Lugols): 6 months
  • Bacterial: 24 hours (must be processed promptly)
  • Metals: up to 6 months (if acidified)
Chain of Custody & lab submission

When sending samples to a laboratory (like Analytical Services Tasmania), you need a Chain of Custody (CoC) form. This documents:

  • Who collected the samples
  • When and where they were collected
  • Every person who handled the samples before they reached the lab

The CoC ensures the integrity of the samples — it proves they haven't been tampered with. It's legally important if results are used for compliance or legal proceedings.

Label each sample with: Site name, date, time of collection, and the tests required. Group samples from each site together in a labelled bag.

Submitting to AST: Complete the submission form (available from AST website). Include hazard information if samples contain toxins, hydrocarbons, or pathogens.

Before you leave the field:
✓ All results recorded on data sheets
✓ Samples correctly labelled and stored (cool/dark)
✓ Rubbish collected and disposed of properly
✓ Any urgent results (e.g., very low DO, extreme pH) reported to supervisor
✓ Equipment cleaned and packed

Interpreting water quality results

Understanding what your results mean is just as important as taking the measurements. Here's how to make sense of typical readings.

Common issues & what they mean
  • Low DO + high nutrients + algal bloom: Classic eutrophication — likely from fertiliser runoff or sewage. Algae die and decompose, consuming oxygen.
  • High EC + high turbidity: Possibly sediment from erosion, or saltwater intrusion in coastal areas.
  • pH below 5: Acidic — could be acid mine drainage, acid sulfate soils, or organic acids from peat.
  • pH above 9: Alkaline — often from algal blooms (photosynthesis removes CO₂, raising pH) or industrial pollution.
  • High nitrate + high phosphate: Likely from agricultural runoff, urban stormwater, or sewage treatment plant discharge.
Diurnal and seasonal variation

Water quality changes throughout the day and across seasons. For example:

  • Day-night: In a shallow lake with algae, pH and DO will rise during the day (photosynthesis) and fall at night (respiration).
  • Seasonal: Temperature is higher in summer, which lowers DO capacity. Summer storms can increase turbidity and nutrient loads.
  • After rainfall: Turbidity and nutrients often spike due to runoff.
Always note the time and date — this context is critical for interpreting results. A low DO reading at 6am in summer might be normal; the same reading at 2pm is a concern.
When to report urgently:
• DO < 60% saturation (or < 4 mg/L)
• pH < 5.0 or > 9.0
• Turbidity > 100 NTU (suspected sediment pollution)
• Visible algal scum or fish kill
• Suspected chemical spill (unusual colour, smell, or foam)
